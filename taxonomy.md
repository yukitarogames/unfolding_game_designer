# Taxonomy

## Incremental Game

An incremental game is a game in which the player builds out their economy and their power.

The thrill of the game is that **you cover a lot of distance** — you get much stronger. There are many, many things to unlock and many, many challenges, and a combination of things you can upgrade, build, or improve helps you get there faster.

> If you start a game session in the morning, what seems like weeks away might be minutes away by evening, because your ability to produce improves very rapidly.

## Unfolding Incremental Game

A genre in which **the scope revealed to the player at the start is much smaller than the full scope**.

- As you play, more ways and more things are revealed to you, so there is a feeling that the scope grows over time.
- Generally, things refer to each other. If you start farming and then unlock fishing, fishing would help you when you farm.

---

## Mechanic

A **mechanic** is a set of rules that refer to each other and enable actions and progress in the game.

- **What bounds a mechanic:** usually its items, its currencies, and its main progression arc.
- **Currencies:** you can buy things in a mechanic with currency from another mechanic, but the currency (or currencies) a mechanic produces will always be usable in that mechanic.
- **Standalone:** a mechanic can be a standalone game.
- **Theme:** a mechanic usually has a theme. Fishing and farming are two different themes, so they are different mechanics.

---

## Unfolding

When you unfold (unlock something new), there are two very different types:

1. **Expansion** — more of an existing mechanic is revealed.
2. **New Mechanic** — a whole new mechanic is revealed.

### Expansion

An expansion reveals another aspect of an existing mechanic. We are still within the same mechanic, but more of it is revealed to us.

When you expand a mechanic, you usually get **another way to progress in that mechanic that has a different engine**.

- A mechanic starts with just a few of its engines, and then expands into more engines.

#### Engine

The engine is what drives a particular way of progressing within a mechanic.

> **Example:** You have a mechanic where you *kill something → get gold → upgrade the thing that kills*. You expand into building labs that research upgrades, and the research is time-gated. Now the engine is **time**, not gold, and you can improve research production.
>
> This is an expansion: we are still trying to get more damage to kill more things — same mechanic — but we have a new way of increasing our damage.

### New Mechanic

Unfolding into a new mechanic:

- **Almost always introduces a new currency.**
- **Almost always introduces a new verb** — killing vs. fishing vs. building.
- **Usually lives in a new tab.** You wouldn't see two mechanics on screen at the same time.
- **Starts from scratch.** If you unfold into farming, you have a tiny farm — one plot where you can plant something. At first it feels like starting a new game.
- **Feeds back.** After some progress in the new mechanic, it benefits your other mechanics (or one specific other mechanic). You go back to it with fresh energy, able to progress where before you were stuck.

Unlocking a new mechanic is a very, very meaningful **promise**.

## Promise

What keeps a player going is **what's coming**. They have a goal they're striving towards.

- Sometimes it's a simple goal, like a big upgrade they know is very strong and will help them progress.
- **The big promises in an unfolding incremental game are expansions and new mechanics.**

### Why a new mechanic is a big promise

After the game gets a bit slow and a bit boring, the player looks at when they'll unlock a new mechanic. They know they'll get two things:

1. **The new-game experience.** They learn something new and get something new. Beginnings progress fast — you buy and unlock things quickly — so they're more fun and more dopamine-heavy.
2. **A return to existing mechanics.** After some progress in the new mechanic, they can go back to an existing one, progress into new territory, and unlock new things.

### Rules for promises

- **Promises must be visible.** The player should know when the next one is coming and what they need to do to get there.
  > e.g. "Clear stage 50 to unlock something." The player doesn't know what it is, but knows it's a new mechanic because the UI taught them mechanics are tabs, and there's a tab with a lock on it.
- **The player doesn't need to know specifically what they're getting.**
- **Promises must be kept.** Don't fail the player. Teach them that promises are kept.
- **A good promise is one where the player is taught that it's something big — and it's always something big.** Then it's very hard to stop playing, because reaching that spot unlocks something fun and consequential.

## Curve

Take a log of a play session that records, with timestamps, whenever the player makes a purchase or unlocks something. **The curve is what the graph of that log looks like.**

A curve can be:

- Spaced out — nothing is bought for a long time.
- Spaced out — nothing is unlocked for a long time.
- Dense.
- Oscillating between fast and slow.

When balancing a game, look at the curve and ask: **Do I like this curve? Is this a good curve?**

### Don't optimize for the curve

What makes a good curve is very tricky. You almost never fine-tune numbers to get a specific curve shape — a precise shape is not important. **The general outline is what matters, and the curve tells you where you have a problem.**

### Fixing a curve

When the curve shows a problem, fix it in one of two ways:

1. **Change a constant.**
   > If enemy HP scales by 1.5× per wave, maybe make it 1.4×.
2. **Introduce a new mechanic or an expansion of an existing mechanic.**
   > When a curve gets too stale — it slows down predictably and nothing happens for a long time — the curve needs to break with an unfolding. What pushes the player through the slowing progress is a target they're pushing towards.

**Don't introduce a lot of new rules to fix the curve** (e.g. "on level 7 I'll change something"). Incremental games have to be **legible**. If you chase a perfect curve you'll make an illegible game where the player doesn't understand how things scale.

### The early game

The beginning needs more density of progress — more buying things and getting more powerful. You don't have to go crazy; you keep the player busy and happy by giving them power.

- **Too slow loses the player.** Click a button, wait 5 minutes, nothing happens → you lose them.
- **Too fast also fails.** If the player just clicks and progresses really fast, you've failed too.

**The real job of the early game is to teach the shape of the game's promises** — the language of the game.

- e.g. *When I unlock a new unit, progress accelerates.*
- e.g. *When I unlock a new tab, I get a fresh mechanic that's really cool and powerful.*

The player should learn what a small, medium, and large promise is, know when each is unlocked, and experience them.

### Retention

Once the player understands the promises and sees the next promise and when they'll reach it, they have a goal to work towards. You get retention when:

- The goal is **reasonably within reach** (not obscene — not "you'll get there in 7 weeks"), and
- The first time that kind of promise was fulfilled, it was a **significant boost in power, interest, and fun**.
